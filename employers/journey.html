Not the usual path

I've been engineering things longer than I've had a job title for it.

On paper, my background can look like several unrelated careers. To me, it's one continuous progression: understand how a system works, discover what it can do, make it useful, and take responsibility when it doesn't.

Different tools, different industries, same problem-solving instinct.

The unofficial family motto
“Well, I guess I'll build one.”

It's been an answer to everything from an improbable childhood project to a missing tool-tracking system. Occasionally, it's also how I get myself into more work.

Age 8 · First experiments

Every broken gadget was a parts department.

I started taking apart radios, electronics, and broken or inherited radio-controlled aircraft when I was about eight. I wanted to know what was inside, why it worked, and what would happen if I made those parts do something else.

Motors, propellers, receivers, bits of wiring, and whatever else survived became improvised airboats and little Rube Goldberg / The Incredible Machine-style contraptions. I wasn't building toward a résumé. I just thought it was incredible that the right combination of physical parts could make something happen.

That early habit taught me to salvage, experiment, troubleshoot, and think of hardware as a collection of capabilities rather than a collection of products.

What stuckHands-on electronics · Mechanical intuition · Salvage and prototyping · Curiosity without instructions
Middle school · Family projects

Then came the rockets.

Middle school brought family rocketry adventures: launches, preparation, experiments, and the rewarding—and occasionally humbling—business of putting something together and seeing whether reality agreed with the plan.

That was an early introduction to a lesson I would encounter repeatedly in professional technical work: a system is only as good as its weakest assumption. Getting the fun result means respecting the preparation, the sequence, and the things you don't completely control.

What stuckTesting · Preparation · Cause and effect · Learning from failures
Age 11 · Amateur radio / KB3PRD

At eleven, I had a call sign—and a whole world of people to learn from.

At eleven years old, I earned my amateur radio license and the call sign KB3PRD. It opened a door that a school classroom couldn't quite replicate: the Keystone VHF Club, where I found generations of technical knowledge and people willing to share it with a curious kid who wanted to know how everything worked.

My interests quickly stretched beyond simply talking into a microphone. There were FM voice satellite communications, including ISS-related radio activities; APRS and packet-based position reporting; HF and long-distance DX; contests; low-power QRP experimentation; and the wonderfully ambitious idea of EME—using the Moon as a radio reflector. Those interests eventually branched into a little radio astronomy and astrophotography. The common thread was irresistible: signals, physics, electronics, antennas, software, and the possibility of making contact across distances I could barely comprehend.

Just as important as the equipment was the community. I was learning from people with decades of experience in RF, electronics, propagation, and problem-solving. They treated technical curiosity as something worth encouraging, and that mentorship made a lasting impression.

My license eventually expired while I was away touring, and I never renewed it; KB3PRD is a former call sign, not a claim of current licensure. By the time my interests were branching into the more exotic corners of radio, jazz band was taking up plenty of my attention. And, well, I'd also discovered girls. An eleven-year-old ham operator only has so many hours in the day.

What stuckRF and antennas · Satellite communications · APRS / HF / DX · Low-power experimentation · Scientific curiosity · Learning across generations
Teenage years · First industry

I found the backstage world before I finished school.

As a teenager, I found a niche in live entertainment. I started as a basic stagehand: unload the truck, build the equipment, run the cable, move the scenery, and be useful wherever another pair of hands was needed.

What captivated me wasn't only the spectacle. It was the machinery behind it—the audio systems, lighting infrastructure, power distribution, effects, rigging, control systems, logistics, and coordination it took to make a show happen on time.

Every department was another system to learn. I kept asking questions and becoming the person who could work across them, not just inside one specialty.

What stuckCross-disciplinary learning · Safety and responsibility · Team communication · Work under hard deadlines
2013–2014 · OTC Fleet Service

From turning wrenches to tracking the parts behind the work.

Before my touring career took off, I spent 2013–2014 at OTC Fleet Service, starting as a shop helper and progressing into technician work. I gained hands-on experience maintaining and repairing gasoline and diesel fleet vehicles, learned to weld, and saw what it took to keep equipment and vehicles ready for the people depending on them.

But the machinery wasn't the only thing I paid attention to. A shop runs on more than mechanical skill: parts, consumables, availability, organization, and the ability to find the right thing when it is needed all matter. I took that problem seriously enough to build a parts-and-consumables tracking system for the shop. It remained in use for years after I built it.

Looking back, that was an early version of a pattern I'd return to later: learn the physical workflow, find the friction in the operation around it, and create a practical system to make the work easier. Long before I was building inventory applications and Linux-hosted tools, I was already thinking about how to connect real equipment, real people, and reliable information.

What stuckGasoline and diesel fleet maintenance · Welding · Shop operations · Parts and consumables tracking · Practical process improvement · Building tools that last
2014–2018 · Touring & events

My classroom got significantly louder.

The next chapter was North American touring and major festivals, especially through 2014–2018. I worked across large-scale live events, festival infrastructure, and high-pressure productions, including work associated with presidential campaigns.

The assignments demanded that I understand and troubleshoot audio, lighting, pyrotechnics and special effects, scenic and mechanical systems, and the logistics connecting them. Different crews, different vendors, different sites—and the expectation that everything would be ready when the doors opened.

Live production is an unforgiving classroom. You learn to diagnose failures quickly, communicate clearly, make tradeoffs, and understand what one subsystem's problem means for everybody else. You also learn what leadership actually looks like at 2 a.m. in the rain when the schedule isn't moving.

What stuckSystems integration · Field diagnostics · Incident response · Leadership · Large-scale coordination
2018 · FPV flight & electronics

The aircraft I'd wanted to build since childhood finally took flight.

I'd wanted to build and fly FPV drones since I was a kid. For years, the missing ingredient wasn't the tools, the ability, or the motivation—it was the disposable income. In 2018, I finally had the means to turn that childhood fascination into working aircraft.

I'd spent years in flight simulators long before I could afford the hardware. That practice paid off the moment I started flying: I already had an intuitive feel for fixed-wing and rotorcraft flight dynamics, control inputs, and what an aircraft would do next. The leap from simulated flight to a machine I'd assembled myself was extraordinary.

Building FPV aircraft brought together the things I'd loved since I was eight: electronics, mechanical assembly, radio systems, soldering, troubleshooting, and the physics of flight. Watching an airframe go from parts on a bench to something I could actually pilot through the sky felt like a technological marvel—and it still does.

What stuckFlight dynamics · Simulator-to-hardware transfer · Radio systems · Electronics integration · Hands-on experimentation
2020 onward · 3D Makers Warehouse

A hobby became a business. Then the business became a technical education.

When the pandemic hit, live entertainment effectively stopped. I got into 3D printing at first because it would expand the things I could make for my own hobbies, including aircraft and electronics projects. I soon wondered if I could turn that capability into a living—but the market for selling printed objects was already crowded.

What wasn't readily available locally was a place to buy printer parts, filament, machines, and knowledgeable help when something went wrong. So I started 3D Makers Warehouse (3DMW) as what I understood to be the region's first—and at the time only—dedicated 3D-printer sales, service, and supply shop. It wasn't just a print farm. We built a brand, a retail and service operation, vendor relationships, a customer base, and a functioning business around supporting other people who wanted to make things.

Early on, I deliberately moved up the local customer chain rather than competing only for one-off hobby orders. We supplied school districts with filament and equipment, built or customized printers for their needs and sold them at a sustainable markup, and helped educators learn to operate and maintain the machines. We also served substantial business-to-business customers whose identities and work I keep confidential under nondisclosure agreements.

The more capable our customers became, the deeper I had to go. I found myself troubleshooting alongside—and occasionally having wonderfully technical conversations with—materials scientists, process engineers, systems administrators, and teachers. Supporting machines properly pushed me beyond mechanical hardware into G-code, printer firmware, Marlin, Klipper, and ultimately Linux. Learning to understand the software underneath the machinery wasn't a side quest anymore; it was part of delivering a dependable product and service.

That decision to serve customers with serious operational needs paid dividends, but it also changed how I thought. Sales, inventory, margins, quoting, training, support, and technical systems weren't separate departments in my head. They were interconnected parts of a business I had helped build.

What stuckFinding underserved markets · Retail and B2B operations · School-district procurement and training · Custom machine builds · Materials and process conversations · G-code / Marlin / Klipper / Linux
Recent years · Operations systems

I tried bringing all those lessons back into the industry.

Returning to technical operations with a broader perspective, I saw recurring opportunities to improve tool accountability, equipment readiness, purchasing, inventory movement, and the flow of information between the people doing the work and the people making decisions.

I took on practical coordination responsibilities—serialized tools, road kits, reconciliations and resupply—and began building software to address the friction directly. That work connected physical workflows to databases, barcodes, service interfaces, reports, and measurable operating outcomes.

I discovered something important: I wasn't only solving equipment problems anymore. I was thinking in terms of systems architecture, process design, software integration, and business decisions.

What stuckOperational data · Inventory and asset systems · Requirements gathering · Workflow automation · Measurable improvements
Now · Hardware meets software

Software finally caught up with what I could imagine.

I've always understood physical and electronic systems intuitively. Software was the gap between the hardware I could envision and the behavior I wanted it to perform. Modern development tools, including AI-assisted coding, gave me another way across that gap.

Rather than stopping at an idea, I began designing, building, testing, deploying, and debugging the applications that let those systems operate: inventory and equipment software, Linux-hosted services, self-hosted media infrastructure, a local AI assistant with real hardware integrations, and interconnected games with persistent shared state.

I'm self-taught in much of this work. I use AI as a development aid, but I own the requirements, the physical integration, the testing, the failures, and the result. What matters is that the solution works and that I can explain it.

What stuckPython / FastAPI · SQL databases · Linux services · Automation · Hardware/software integration · End-to-end delivery

The next chapter

I'm not leaving engineering.
I'm expanding where I can apply it.

Live entertainment taught me more about systems, urgency, people, and responsibility than I could have learned sitting still. I value that foundation. But I've reached a point where the problems I most want to solve aren't limited to a shop floor, a production schedule, or a truck full of road cases.

I want to bring that experience into a remote-first, growth-oriented technical role where I can design better workflows, build and support useful systems, solve larger operational problems, and be appropriately compensated for the scope of responsibility.

My background doesn't look like a conventional software engineer's, NOC technician's, systems analyst's, or operations manager's. That's also why I can connect the pieces between those roles. I understand the machinery, the people using it, the business paying for it, and increasingly, the software that ties it all together.
